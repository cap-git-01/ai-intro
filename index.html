08 / Execution

Three execution patterns

Not everything needs an agent — and not every AI workflow needs an open-ended loop.

→

1. Deterministic automation

Trigger → inputs → fixed logic → output.

Example: scheduled validation pipeline
◇

2. AI-enhanced workflow

A defined workflow includes an AI interpretation or classification step.

Example: categorize exceptions
↻

3. Agentic execution

A goal-directed agent chooses next steps based on observed results.

Example: investigate unknown root cause
Use deterministic logic where rules are known; use AI judgment where ambiguity or changing conditions warrant it.

10 / Adoption

Four levels of AI adoption

A teaching framework for increasing integration and autonomy — not a universal industry standard.

011 · AssistantA person asks AI for help.
022 · AI-enhanced workflowAI performs a defined step in a repeatable process.
033 · Agentic investigationAI chooses and iterates through investigative steps.
044 · Governed autonomyAI monitors and acts within explicit authority, with review where needed.
Higher maturity does not mean replacing deterministic systems with AI. It means using intelligence appropriately within reliable processes.

13 / Governance

The queue as a governance layer

Agent suggestions become reviewable proposals — not immediate changes.

01DetectIdentify candidate issues or actions.
02ClassifyGroup by common rule, evidence, and risk.
03QueueStore proposed changes with provenance and impact.
04AuthorizeApprove, reject, or escalate — individually or in batches.
05Execute & verifyApply through deterministic services and check outcomes.
For batch approval, bind authorization to an immutable proposed change set; recheck if underlying records have changed.

14 / The scaling problem

What happens when many agents work?

Individual intelligence is useful. Shared interpretation is what makes coordinated intelligence reliable.

≠

Different definitions

Two agents calculate “active customer” differently.

Conflicting results
⇄

Different systems

A “provider” in one application means something else in another.

Ambiguous relationships
◎

More autonomy

Inconsistent interpretations can become inconsistent actions.

Greater operational risk
More agents multiply the value of shared meaning — and the cost of inconsistent meaning.

16 / The conclusion

From AI tools to trusted intelligence

Models are increasingly capable. The enduring challenge is helping many systems understand and act on the same reality.

AI provides intelligence.
Agents put it to work.
Shared meaning lets it scale.
A governed semantic foundation can amplify reuse, consistency, and safe automation — potentially dramatically, but not automatically or by a guaranteed factor.
